The Pursuit of Compounding      Jun 15, 2026                   33             12          10      Share             On June 11 Adobe released its Q2 2026 report, beating on the top line, raising its full-year revenue target, and yet the market was quite unhappy, sending shares further into the red.   It’s now a very familiar formula for any Adobe investor: Beat + Raise = Selloff.   On the surface it seems the market is wrong - Adobe has posted six consecutive beat-and-raise quarters. And yet, since 2021 shares have been selling off, and are now back to 2018 prices.   Is the market being stubborn? Or, just like the newspaper stocks of past, is the market being prescient, front-running the eventual decline in fundamentals?  This post builds on our Q1 2026 update.            Adobe Q1 2026: Cracks in the Compounder?    The Pursuit of Compounding   ·  Mar 17      Read full story                                     Subscribe          Q2 2026 Financials                                                    Income and Cash Flow Statements                The top line is still compounding at a low-double-digit clip: Q2 revenue grew 12.7% year over year to $6.62 billion (about 11% in constant currency), first-half revenue rose 12.3% to $13.02 billion, and total ARR reached $27.10 billion, up 12.5%.                                       EPS looks stronger than that, and it is worth understanding why. GAAP diluted EPS grew 7.9% in the quarter to $4.25 and non-GAAP grew 17.8% to $5.96, but GAAP net income itself was essentially flat, up just 1.2% to $1.712 billion, because the tax rate jumped to 23.5% from roughly 19.5% and operating margins compressed.   Almost all of the GAAP EPS growth came from the denominator: the diluted share count fell about 6.3% year over year, from 429 million to 402 million. In other words, buybacks, not operating earnings, are currently carrying reported per-share growth.                                       Free cash flow was the one number that did not grow. Operating cash flow was $2.165 billion in the quarter versus $2.191 billion a year ago, and after roughly $58 million of capex, free cash flow of about $2.1 billion was flat to slightly down year over year.                                       Free cash flow is a direct casualty of the expansion of the freemium model - expenses all rose while monetization is delayed (more on that below).   On buybacks, Adobe actually pulled back: it repurchased $2.111 billion of stock in Q2, down from $3.5 billion in the prior-year quarter, almost certainly to preserve cash for the $1.56 billion Semrush acquisition that closed in the period. The buyback is still large enough to shrink the share count meaningfully, but the pace decelerated, and that matters because the buyback is the engine doing most of the EPS work right now.  Balance Sheet                As of May 29, 2026, Adobe held $5.63 billion in cash and short-term investments against $6.65 billion of debt (about $7.07 billion including operating leases), leaving net debt of only around $1 billion against a business that generates roughly $9 billion of free cash flow a year.   Debt did tick up over the half, from $6.21 billion at the November year-end to $6.65 billion, with $1.84 billion now classified as current (it was zero at year-end), and the cash flow statement shows $493 million of new debt issued in the quarter. The natural worry, given the simultaneous buybacks, is that Adobe is borrowing to repurchase stock, and the more notorious version of that pattern is a real red flag.                                       However operating cash flow of $2.165 billion nearly fully covered the $2.111 billion of buybacks on its own and the incremental debt and the $1.4 billion drawdown in the cash balance went toward funding the Semrush acquisition, not the repurchases.  None of that is a solvency or liquidity concern. It is a quality-of-earnings concern, which is a different and more relevant thing for an owner at this price.    Share    A Deliberate ARR Sacrifice                 The company is pushing hard on freemium distribution. Monthly active users across Acrobat and Express moved past 850 million, up from roughly 700 million. Creative freemium MAU passed 90 million, up from around 50 million. Management was direct about the cost of this on the earnings call: the strategy   “will come at the cost of short-term ARR,”   and it   “lowers our second-half ARR growth expectations from individual subscribers.”   They are trading near-term recurring revenue for a much larger top of funnel.   There is a coherent strategy under this - get a digital-native generation using Acrobat and Express for free, build the habit, and convert a fraction of an enormous base later at a high lifetime value.   If it works, the 850 million number is the seed of the next decade of subscriber growth. If it does not, Adobe has handed away pricing power and ARR growth in exchange for usage that never monetizes.   The investor cannot distinguish between the two outcomes from the outside, because Adobe has not yet given us cohort conversion data on the freemium base. Perhaps unsurprisingly, the market is penalizing the cost (slower ARR) more than it is crediting the strategy (a 940-million-user funnel). That is a reasonable thing for the market to do, as there is no guaranteed pay off.   It is also exactly the kind of intentional, long-horizon trade that gets punished in the short run and occasionally looks brilliant in hindsight. One rub is that the long term outcome depends on management execution, and management is turning over (more below).   Growth Quality and Semrush                Although ARR grew year over year, Adobe also closed its acquisition of Semrush during the period. When a company posts low-double-digit growth and has just bolted on an acquisition, the first thing an owner should do is ask what the organic number looks like underneath, as prior to close it was decelerating.                                                                                                             Shared by @HatedMoats on Substack Notes      “The strategic shift to acquire more freemium customers through Acrobat and Firefly lowers our second half ARR growth expectations from individual subscribers. We believe these changes do make Adobe even stronger.”      - Shantanu Narayen (CEO), Q2 2026 Earnings call    An Empty CFO Chair, No Insider Buying                What makes the investment thesis even harder is more C-Suite turnover.  Last quarter it was announced that CEO Shantanu Narayen is transitioning to Board Chair, with a successor search underway. As of this quarter, Dan Durn - who has been CFO since 2021 - is also leaving. Steve Day is stepping in as interim CFO.  Both of the two roles most responsible for strategy development, capital allocation and the margin trajectory are in transition at once, during the most aggressive strategic pivot Adobe has attempted in a decade.   Durn leaving is probably not a red flag for hidden problems; perhaps he knows he’s been passed over for the CEO job. However it is nonetheless a real cost, and the discontinuity in the C-Suite creates strategy and execution risk. Markets discount uncertainty, and an empty CFO chair during a freemium pivot and a margin wobble is a legitimate reason for an investor to sell.   Finally, insiders have been net sellers over the past 12 months. If the company is strong, and being ridiculously discounted by the market, then why aren’t insiders scooping it up hand over fist?                                     InsiderScreener.com    Reassessing the Scorecard                 In our   January 2026   post we outlined three KPIs to watch for structural decay:       RPO (Remaining Performance Obligations):   If this metric starts to decline, then it’s a clear sign Enterprise spend is looking elsewhere.        AI-influenced Annual Recurring Revenue (AI-ARR):   If Adobe is truly integrating AI into their product offerings, then investors will see a clear measure of AI’s direct contribution to its recurring revenue.         Operating Margins:   If margins compress and stay compressed, then it implies Adobe is spending too much on compute to deliver AI features without having the pricing power to pass those costs on to customers.      Here is where they stand exiting Q2.  ✅Remaining Performance Obligations (RPO)                RPO is contracted future revenue that customers are legally obligated to pay. It grew 13% year over year to $22.27 billion, with current RPO (the portion due within twelve months) steady at 67% of the total.                                       This remains the strongest piece of evidence against the &quot;enterprise displacement&quot; narrative. Corporations are still signing massive, long-term contracts with Adobe. Furthermore, the number of enterprise customers generating over $10 million in ARR grew by more than 20% year-over-year.  As we wrote in the last quarter update:    “The expansion of the RPO backlog refutes the most extreme bearish theories that enterprise Chief Information Officers (CIOs) are halting Adobe deployments in favor of upstart AI alternatives. If enterprises truly believed that Adobe’s suite was imminently replaceable by a generic text-to-image prompt, they would not be locking themselves into long-term, multi-million-dollar contractual obligations.”    The enterprise segment continues to show resilience in the face of AI-risk.   ✅ AI-Influenced Annual Recurring Revenue (AI-ARR)                AI monetization is the second piece of good news. AI-first ARR crossed $500 million, up roughly threefold year over year. Firefly’s ending ARR is approaching $300 million and grew about 50% sequentially. Customer Experience Orchestration AI-first ARR grew fourfold. Adobe is undeniably proving it can charge for AI directly.    &quot;Adobe’s AI innovation has driven an impressive 3X year over year increase in AI-first ARR to greater than $500 million. We believe now is the time to aggressively acquire the next generation of Adobe loyalists.&quot;     - Shantanu Narayen (CEO), Q2 2026 Earnings call    This is still small relative to a $27 billion ARR base, but the slope matters more than the level at this stage, and the slope is steep. The fear a year ago was that AI would be the thing that killed Adobe. The early data says Adobe is, so far, selling AI rather than being disintermediated by it.  The franchise is intact, the contracted backlog is growing, and the AI products are being bought.   All good, right?  ❌ Operating Margin Compression                Although two of the three KPIs are trending in a good direction, operating margin has begun to wobble.   Although Gross Margins are relatively stable, GAAP operating margin fell to 33.8% in Q2 from 37.8% in Q1. Non-GAAP operating margin dropped to 44.5% from 47.4%.                                      Adobe also recorded a $70 million goodwill impairment is tied to the Publishing &amp; Advertising unit. Because it is a non-cash charge, it does not impact Adobe's actual liquidity or its $2.17 billion in Q2 operating cash flow.   However, the significance lies in what it signals: it is an accounting acknowledgment that the value of past acquisitions or legacy assets in that specific advertising unit has permanently decreased. Much like the unexpected decline in the traditional Adobe Stock business in Q1 2026, this impairment charge is another structural &quot;yellow flag&quot; confirming that certain legacy, pre-AI revenue streams are facing friction and decay.  Even if we strip out that impairment the operating margins are still declining. Remember the flat free cash flow growth? Although Adobe is successfully generating more revenue, it is currently spending almost all of that incremental revenue on AI development, marketing, and the computing power needed to support nearly a billion free or lower-tier users in hopes of monetizing them down the road.  This Q2 drop is the first quantitative &quot;crack&quot; indicating that the massive compute costs of supporting 90 million creative freemium users and 850 million Acrobat/Express users may be starting to compress profitability.   To keep the &quot;friction-free&quot; onboarding experience for these new users, management actively chose to defer   &quot;previously planned Creative Cloud second-half line optimizations&quot;   (price hikes).     The bearish read here is that Adobe   can't   raise prices on its core Creative Cloud base because cheaper AI alternatives (like Canva and Midjourney) are finally capping their pricing power.     Share    A Bifurcation in the Business                Adobe reports along two customer groups - Business Professionals &amp; Consumers, and Creative &amp; Marketing Professionals - we’re going to reorganize it as durable (Enterprise user group) vs. disrupted (Freemium / Prosumer user group). Breaking it down we see the stark monetization bifurcation emerging between these two groups.   We see the Enterprise group is growing - RPO continues to trend up, and the number of Enterprise customers generating over $10 million ARR grew by more than 20% year-over-year. This shows that Adobe is successfully embedding itself deeper into corporate workflows, with AI-driven enterprise solutions like GenStudio growing over 25% year-over-year and Adobe Experience Platform (AEP) growing over 30%. The recent Semrush acquisition, which added $480 million in ARR, is actively deepening this enterprise moat and workflow stickiness. Adobe has no problems capturing and monetizing these users.  In the Freemium / Prosumer group we see that users are not fleeing to competitors; rather, Adobe is deferring monetization to build a massive, long-term user base. Acrobat and Express Monthly Active Users (MAU) have surged past 850 million. Creative freemium MAUs (which includes web and mobile versions of Firefly, Express, Photoshop, and Lightroom) crossed 90 million, representing 80% year-over-year growth.   The bullish read is that Adobe is sacrificing short term margin for user capture. The bearish read is that this is evidence that Adobe has no pricing power.   Furthermore, in Adobe’s legacy, pre-AI segments (e.g. Adobe Stock business, Publishing &amp; Advertising unit) there is clear deterioration in the fundamentals as the industry shifts.  In short, the Enterprise group is acting as a highly profitable, durable anchor. Meanwhile, the Freemium / Prosumer financials look strained, because Adobe is spending its margins today to build a massive freemium funnel it hopes to then monetize in the future.  A Precedent for the Transition                Adobe has gone through a historical transition like this before. Between 2011 and 2013 it stopped selling Creative Suite as a perpetual license and pushed its customers onto Creative Cloud subscriptions. The optics were ugly.  Reported revenue fell, from about $4.4 billion in FY2012 to roughly $4.05 billion in FY2013, because the company was trading large upfront license payments for small monthly ones. Operating margins compressed. Customers signed petitions against the change. For the better part of two years the income statement read like a business in retreat, and plenty of investors read it exactly that way and sold.  However with the benefit of hindsight we see that the falling revenue was the mechanical signature of a value-accretive transition rather than evidence of decay, and the investors who understood that watched deferred revenue and net subscriber adds instead of the headline numbers. Eventually, they were paid handsomely for the patience.   At that time the skeptics had effectively handed Adobe a three-or-four-year future and priced it for decline, and they were wrong because they confused the optics of a model transition with the erosion of a franchise. Sound familiar?  Fast forward to present day, and Adobe is once again choosing to make a near-term number worse in service of a larger long-run model. In 2013 it sacrificed upfront license revenue for recurring subscription revenue. In 2026 it is sacrificing near-term ARR for a freemium funnel of more than 900 million users. The shape is the same: a deliberate, management-chosen sacrifice, ugly optics, compressing margins, a skeptical market that penalizes the near-term certainty, more than it credits the long term possible benefits. In 2013 that indicator was deferred revenue. Today it is RPO and freemium conversion.  So is it the same movie? Almost. There is one place where the 2013 script and the 2026 script diverge, and that’s who is in charge. Shantanu Narayen was the CEO who saw Adobe through that transition, and he is choosing to leave as Adobe begins it’s next transition.    Buy? Hold? Sell?                The bear case is clear and has merit. Organic ARR is decelerating, the quality of earnings is changing, management is building a freemium funnel that has not yet proven it converts, operating margin is compressing while AI compute scales, and the two executives best placed to manage it all are turning over at the same time. Every one of these is real and happening, and clearly the market doesn’t like it.  The bull case is that Enterprise is strong, and is showing signs that it is getting stronger. Enterprise consumers are buying Adobe’s AI products (AI-ARR is growing), RPO continues to be strong, and the Semrush acquisition deepens the value proposition of the Adobe offering.   It’s easy to see how a shareholder would have waning conviction. There is clear erosion in parts of the business. Perhaps most importantly, the two most important executives are turning over, and there has been no insider buying. Yes, the Enterprise business may be resilient, but is that enough?   There is at least a short term opportunity cost to buying and holding. Although the shares appear to be undervalued the technicals would suggest the bottom is not yet in, and the capital put to work in Adobe could probably be utilized elsewhere.   On the other hand, the lower the price goes, the more the future investment case is de-risked and the higher the potential future return.   Ultimately, our decision is to hold and watch. We aren’t adding here, instead, we will accept that Adobe is becoming a smaller overall percentage of our portfolio. Two of our three KPIs are strong, while one is starting to wobble. If the KPIs weaken further in the coming quarters, then we will sell. and yet the market was quite unhappy, sending shares further into the red.   It\u2019s now a very familiar formula for any Adobe investor: Beat + Raise = Selloff.   On the surface it seems the market is wrong - Adobe has posted six consecutive beat-and-raise quarters. And yet, since 2021 shares have been selling off, and are now back to 2018 prices.   Is the market being stubborn? Or, just like the newspaper stocks of past, is the market being prescient, front-running the eventual decline in fundamentals?  This post builds on our Q1 2026 update.      Subscribe now    Q2 2026 Financials                                      Income and Cash Flow Statements  The top line is still compounding at a low-double-digit clip: Q2 revenue grew 12.7% year over year to $6.62 billion (about 11% in constant currency), first-half revenue rose 12.3% to $13.02 billion, and total ARR reached $27.10 billion, up 12.5%.                                       EPS looks stronger than that, and it is worth understanding why. GAAP diluted EPS grew 7.9% in the quarter to $4.25 and non-GAAP grew 17.8% to $5.96, but GAAP net income itself was essentially flat, up just 1.2% to $1.712 billion, because the tax rate jumped to 23.5% from roughly 19.5% and operating margins compressed.   Almost all of the GAAP EPS growth came from the denominator: the diluted share count fell about 6.3% year over year, from 429 million to 402 million. In other words, buybacks, not operating earnings, are currently carrying reported per-share growth.                                       Free cash flow was the one number that did not grow. Operating cash flow was $2.165 billion in the quarter versus $2.191 billion a year ago, and after roughly $58 million of capex, free cash flow of about $2.1 billion was flat to slightly down year over year.                                       Free cash flow is a direct casualty of the expansion of the freemium model - expenses all rose while monetization is delayed (more on that below).   On buybacks, Adobe actually pulled back: it repurchased $2.111 billion of stock in Q2, down from $3.5 billion in the prior-year quarter, almost certainly to preserve cash for the $1.56 billion Semrush acquisition that closed in the period. The buyback is still large enough to shrink the share count meaningfully, but the pace decelerated, and that matters because the buyback is the engine doing most of the EPS work right now.  Balance Sheet  As of May 29, 2026, Adobe held $5.63 billion in cash and short-term investments against $6.65 billion of debt (about $7.07 billion including operating leases), leaving net debt of only around $1 billion against a business that generates roughly $9 billion of free cash flow a year.   Debt did tick up over the half, from $6.21 billion at the November year-end to $6.65 billion, with $1.84 billion now classified as current (it was zero at year-end), and the cash flow statement shows $493 million of new debt issued in the quarter. The natural worry, given the simultaneous buybacks, is that Adobe is borrowing to repurchase stock, and the more notorious version of that pattern is a real red flag.                                       However operating cash flow of $2.165 billion nearly fully covered the $2.111 billion of buybacks on its own and the incremental debt and the $1.4 billion drawdown in the cash balance went toward funding the Semrush acquisition, not the repurchases.  None of that is a solvency or liquidity concern. It is a quality-of-earnings concern, which is a different and more relevant thing for an owner at this price.    Share    A Deliberate ARR Sacrifice  The company is pushing hard on freemium distribution. Monthly active users across Acrobat and Express moved past 850 million, up from roughly 700 million. Creative freemium MAU passed 90 million, up from around 50 million. Management was direct about the cost of this on the earnings call: the strategy  \u201Cwill come at the cost of short-term ARR,\u201D  and it  \u201Clowers our second-half ARR growth expectations from individual subscribers.\u201D  They are trading near-term recurring revenue for a much larger top of funnel.  There is a coherent strategy under this - get a digital-native generation using Acrobat and Express for free, build the habit, and convert a fraction of an enormous base later at a high lifetime value.   If it works, the 850 million number is the seed of the next decade of subscriber growth. If it does not, Adobe has handed away pricing power and ARR growth in exchange for usage that never monetizes.   The investor cannot distinguish between the two outcomes from the outside, because Adobe has not yet given us cohort conversion data on the freemium base. Perhaps unsurprisingly, the market is penalizing the cost (slower ARR) more than it is crediting the strategy (a 940-million-user funnel). That is a reasonable thing for the market to do, as there is no guaranteed pay off.   It is also exactly the kind of intentional, long-horizon trade that gets punished in the short run and occasionally looks brilliant in hindsight. One rub is that the long term outcome depends on management execution, and management is turning over (more below).   Growth Quality and Semrush  Although ARR grew year over year, Adobe also closed its acquisition of Semrush during the period. When a company posts low-double-digit growth and has just bolted on an acquisition, the first thing an owner should do is ask what the organic number looks like underneath, as prior to close it was decelerating. We believe these changes do make Adobe even stronger.\u201D    - Shantanu Narayen (CEO), Q2 2026 Earnings call    An Empty CFO Chair, No Insider Buying  What makes the investment thesis even harder is more C-Suite turnover.  Last quarter it was announced that CEO Shantanu Narayen is transitioning to Board Chair, with a successor search underway. As of this quarter, Dan Durn - who has been CFO since 2021 - is also leaving. Steve Day is stepping in as interim CFO.  Both of the two roles most responsible for strategy development, capital allocation and the margin trajectory are in transition at once, during the most aggressive strategic pivot Adobe has attempted in a decade.   Durn leaving is probably not a red flag for hidden problems; perhaps he knows he\u2019s been passed over for the CEO job. However it is nonetheless a real cost, and the discontinuity in the C-Suite creates strategy and execution risk. Markets discount uncertainty, and an empty CFO chair during a freemium pivot and a margin wobble is a legitimate reason for an investor to sell.   Finally, insiders have been net sellers over the past 12 months. If the company is strong, and being ridiculously discounted by the market, then why aren\u2019t insiders scooping it up hand over fist?                                     InsiderScreener.com    Reassessing the Scorecard  In our  January 2026  post we outlined three KPIs to watch for structural decay:      RPO (Remaining Performance Obligations):  If this metric starts to decline, then it\u2019s a clear sign Enterprise spend is looking elsewhere.       AI-influenced Annual Recurring Revenue (AI-ARR):  If Adobe is truly integrating AI into their product offerings, then investors will see a clear measure of AI\u2019s direct contribution to its recurring revenue.        Operating Margins:  If margins compress and stay compressed, then it implies Adobe is spending too much on compute to deliver AI features without having the pricing power to pass those costs on to customers.     Here is where they stand exiting Q2.  \u2705Remaining Performance Obligations (RPO)  RPO is contracted future revenue that customers are legally obligated to pay. It grew 13% year over year to $22.27 billion, with current RPO (the portion due within twelve months) steady at 67% of the total.                                       This remains the strongest piece of evidence against the \\\"enterprise displacement\\\" narrative. Corporations are still signing massive, long-term contracts with Adobe. Furthermore, the number of enterprise customers generating over $10 million in ARR grew by more than 20% year-over-year.  As we wrote in the last quarter update:    \u201CThe expansion of the RPO backlog refutes the most extreme bearish theories that enterprise Chief Information Officers (CIOs) are halting Adobe deployments in favor of upstart AI alternatives. If enterprises truly believed that Adobe\u2019s suite was imminently replaceable by a generic text-to-image prompt, they would not be locking themselves into long-term, multi-million-dollar contractual obligations.\u201D    The enterprise segment continues to show resilience in the face of AI-risk.   \u2705 AI-Influenced Annual Recurring Revenue (AI-ARR)  AI monetization is the second piece of good news. AI-first ARR crossed $500 million, up roughly threefold year over year. Firefly\u2019s ending ARR is approaching $300 million and grew about 50% sequentially. Customer Experience Orchestration AI-first ARR grew fourfold. Adobe is undeniably proving it can charge for AI directly.    \\\"Adobe\u2019s AI innovation has driven an impressive 3X year over year increase in AI-first ARR to greater than $500 million. We believe now is the time to aggressively acquire the next generation of Adobe loyalists.\\\"   - Shantanu Narayen (CEO), Q2 2026 Earnings call    This is still small relative to a $27 billion ARR base, but the slope matters more than the level at this stage, and the slope is steep. The fear a year ago was that AI would be the thing that killed Adobe. The early data says Adobe is, so far, selling AI rather than being disintermediated by it.  The franchise is intact, the contracted backlog is growing, and the AI products are being bought.   All good, right?  \u274C Operating Margin Compression  Although two of the three KPIs are trending in a good direction, operating margin has begun to wobble.   Although Gross Margins are relatively stable, GAAP operating margin fell to 33.8% in Q2 from 37.8% in Q1. Non-GAAP operating margin dropped to 44.5% from 47.4%.                                      Adobe also recorded a $70 million goodwill impairment is tied to the Publishing &amp; Advertising unit. Because it is a non-cash charge, it does not impact Adobe's actual liquidity or its $2.17 billion in Q2 operating cash flow.   However, the significance lies in what it signals: it is an accounting acknowledgment that the value of past acquisitions or legacy assets in that specific advertising unit has permanently decreased. Much like the unexpected decline in the traditional Adobe Stock business in Q1 2026, this impairment charge is another structural \\\"yellow flag\\\" confirming that certain legacy, pre-AI revenue streams are facing friction and decay.  Even if we strip out that impairment the operating margins are still declining. Remember the flat free cash flow growth? Although Adobe is successfully generating more revenue, it is currently spending almost all of that incremental revenue on AI development, marketing, and the computing power needed to support nearly a billion free or lower-tier users in hopes of monetizing them down the road.  This Q2 drop is the first quantitative \\\"crack\\\" indicating that the massive compute costs of supporting 90 million creative freemium users and 850 million Acrobat/Express users may be starting to compress profitability.  To keep the \\\"friction-free\\\" onboarding experience for these new users, management actively chose to defer  \\\"previously planned Creative Cloud second-half line optimizations\\\"  (price hikes).   The bearish read here is that Adobe  can't  raise prices on its core Creative Cloud base because cheaper AI alternatives (like Canva and Midjourney) are finally capping their pricing power.    Share    A Bifurcation in the Business  Adobe reports along two customer groups - Business Professionals &amp; Consumers, and Creative &amp; Marketing Professionals - we\u2019re going to reorganize it as durable (Enterprise user group) vs. disrupted (Freemium / Prosumer user group). Breaking it down we see the stark monetization bifurcation emerging between these two groups.   We see the Enterprise group is growing - RPO continues to trend up, and the number of Enterprise customers generating over $10 million ARR grew by more than 20% year-over-year. This shows that Adobe is successfully embedding itself deeper into corporate workflows, with AI-driven enterprise solutions like GenStudio growing over 25% year-over-year and Adobe Experience Platform (AEP) growing over 30%. The recent Semrush acquisition, which added $480 million in ARR, is actively deepening this enterprise moat and workflow stickiness. Adobe has no problems capturing and monetizing these users.  In the Freemium / Prosumer group we see that users are not fleeing to competitors; rather, Adobe is deferring monetization to build a massive, long-term user base. Acrobat and Express Monthly Active Users (MAU) have surged past 850 million. Creative freemium MAUs (which includes web and mobile versions of Firefly, Express, Photoshop, and Lightroom) crossed 90 million, representing 80% year-over-year growth.   The bullish read is that Adobe is sacrificing short term margin for user capture. The bearish read is that this is evidence that Adobe has no pricing power.   Furthermore, in Adobe\u2019s legacy, pre-AI segments (e.g. Adobe Stock business, Publishing &amp; Advertising unit) there is clear deterioration in the fundamentals as the industry shifts.  In short, the Enterprise group is acting as a highly profitable, durable anchor. Meanwhile, the Freemium / Prosumer financials look strained, because Adobe is spending its margins today to build a massive freemium funnel it hopes to then monetize in the future.  A Precedent for the Transition  Adobe has gone through a historical transition like this before. Between 2011 and 2013 it stopped selling Creative Suite as a perpetual license and pushed its customers onto Creative Cloud subscriptions. The optics were ugly.  Reported revenue fell, from about $4.4 billion in FY2012 to roughly $4.05 billion in FY2013, because the company was trading large upfront license payments for small monthly ones. Operating margins compressed. Customers signed petitions against the change. For the better part of two years the income statement read like a business in retreat, and plenty of investors read it exactly that way and sold.  However with the benefit of hindsight we see that the falling revenue was the mechanical signature of a value-accretive transition rather than evidence of decay, and the investors who understood that watched deferred revenue and net subscriber adds instead of the headline numbers. Eventually, they were paid handsomely for the patience.   At that time the skeptics had effectively handed Adobe a three-or-four-year future and priced it for decline, and they were wrong because they confused the optics of a model transition with the erosion of a franchise. Sound familiar?  Fast forward to present day, and Adobe is once again choosing to make a near-term number worse in service of a larger long-run model. In 2013 it sacrificed upfront license revenue for recurring subscription revenue. In 2026 it is sacrificing near-term ARR for a freemium funnel of more than 900 million users. The shape is the same: a deliberate, management-chosen sacrifice, ugly optics, compressing margins, a skeptical market that penalizes the near-term certainty, more than it credits the long term possible benefits. In 2013 that indicator was deferred revenue. Today it is RPO and freemium conversion.  So is it the same movie? Almost. There is one place where the 2013 script and the 2026 script diverge, and that\u2019s who is in charge. Shantanu Narayen was the CEO who saw Adobe through that transition, and he is choosing to leave as Adobe begins it\u2019s next transition.    Buy? Hold? Sell?  The bear case is clear and has merit. Organic ARR is decelerating, the quality of earnings is changing, management is building a freemium funnel that has not yet proven it converts, operating margin is compressing while AI compute scales, and the two executives best placed to manage it all are turning over at the same time. Every one of these is real and happening, and clearly the market doesn\u2019t like it.  The bull case is that Enterprise is strong, and is showing signs that it is getting stronger. Enterprise consumers are buying Adobe\u2019s AI products (AI-ARR is growing), RPO continues to be strong, and the Semrush acquisition deepens the value proposition of the Adobe offering.   It\u2019s easy to see how a shareholder would have waning conviction. There is clear erosion in parts of the business. Perhaps most importantly, the two most important executives are turning over, and there has been no insider buying. Yes, the Enterprise business may be resilient, but is that enough?   There is at least a short term opportunity cost to buying and holding. Although the shares appear to be undervalued the technicals would suggest the bottom is not yet in, and the capital put to work in Adobe could probably be utilized elsewhere.   On the other hand, the lower the price goes, the more the future investment case is de-risked and the higher the potential future return.   Ultimately, our decision is to hold and watch. We aren\u2019t adding here, instead, we will accept that Adobe is becoming a smaller overall percentage of our portfolio. Two of our three KPIs are strong, while one is starting to wobble. If the KPIs weaken further in the coming quarters, then we will sell.
